# Proposed Definition: Dreamcore

**Version:** 1.0-draft  
**Status:** proposal for research and voluntary classification; not an established industry standard  
**Date:** 2026-09-24

## Short definition

**Dreamcore** is an interactive work in which a creator can document that one or more of their own dream experiences materially shaped the work's world, narrative, mechanics, events, entities, environments, rules, sound, or overall structure.

The defining test is not “does it look dreamlike?” It is:

> **Can the creator's own dream experience be traced as a material source of the interactive work?**

## Mandatory characteristics

A work must meet all of the following to be classified as Dreamcore under this proposal:

1. **Interactive work:** it is a game or another work whose audience makes meaningful choices or actions, rather than a static image, soundtrack, article, or conventional film alone.
2. **Personal dream source:** at least one source is an actual dream experience of the creator or a clearly identified member of the credited creator team. A fictional dream, generic nightmare, dream diary belonging to someone else, or a dream used only as a mood reference does not automatically satisfy this criterion.
3. **Material influence:** the source must shape at least one central creative axis: world, level, narrative, entity, event, mechanic, rule, sound design, interface, or structure. A single decorative color or vague atmosphere is not enough.
4. **Traceability:** a public or privately held, reviewable record links the source to the work. The record may be a creator statement, interview, design note, journal excerpt with consent, prototype comparison, or a sufficiently specific description. Private details need not be published.
5. **Authorship clarity:** the source is attributed to a creator or team member responsible for the work, and any team use has the consent of the people whose experiences are claimed.

## Optional characteristics

These may occur but are not required:

- a recurring dream or dream journal;
- nightmares, night terrors, sleep paralysis, or false awakenings;
- dream logic, impossible transitions, or distorted time;
- lucidity, dream awareness, or unreliable waking;
- liminal, nostalgic, surreal, uncanny, or “dreamcore” visual language;
- first-person perspective or exploration;
- horror, psychological horror, analog horror, or surreal horror;
- symbolic transformation rather than literal reconstruction;
- a collection of several dreams or one defining dream;
- use of sound, silence, motifs, or absence as the main reconstruction.

## Common characteristics are not requirements

Many examples may share first-person perspective, surreal spaces, a dream narrative, or a liminal aesthetic. Those features are useful comparative axes, not the definition.

## What does not qualify by itself

- A game that only looks dreamlike.
- A game about a fictional character who dreams.
- A game inspired by dreams in general, dream theory, or another person's diary, without evidence of the creator's own dream source.
- A game with generic horror or generic “nightmare” imagery.
- A game inspired by mental illness or trauma when no dream/nightmare provenance is documented.
- A game tagged `dreams`, `nightmare`, `liminal space`, or `dreamcore` on a store.
- A soundtrack, mood board, static illustration, or article that contains dream imagery but is not an interactive work.
- A work whose creator only says “I had dreams” without connecting an experience to the work.

## Symbolism and abstraction

A dream does not need to be copied literally. A creator may transform a recurring image, emotion, spatial rule, sound, or event into a game asset. Classification asks whether the transformation is material and traceable, not whether the player can identify the original dream.

Creators may describe a source as “a recurring feeling of being watched” or “a recurring doorway in my sleep” without disclosing trauma, names, locations, medical information, or the full dream.

## Examples

| Example | Classification under this proposal | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| A short game reconstructed from the creator's documented recurring dream | Dreamcore | Interactive, personal source, material reconstruction |
| A game collection whose levels each transform an entry from the creator's dream journal | Dreamcore | Several documented source experiences shape the work |
| A game with dreamlike visuals but no personal-source evidence | Not classified | Appearance alone fails the mandatory test |
| A psychological horror game inspired by the creator's OCD/depression but not documented as dream-derived | Adjacent, not Dreamcore | Personal experience is not enough without the dream criterion |
| A game based on a collaborator's dream diary with the collaborator credited and consenting | Possible case requiring a team rule | It may fit a future collective-work variant; v1.0 requires careful review |
| A game using a fictional nightmare from a novel | Not Dreamcore | The source is borrowed fiction, not the creator's own dream |

## Relationship to Dreamcore Horror

A work that meets this definition and is materially shaped by frightening dreams, nightmares, or night terrors may also be classified as **Dreamcore Horror**. That branch is defined separately and is not implied by the word “dream.”

## Version and interpretation

This is a **minimum criterion**, not a claim that every possible dream-derived work looks alike. Future versions may add a collective-work rule, an archival provenance standard, or optional subbranches. Any change should preserve the distinction between a creator's own experience and generic dream aesthetics.
